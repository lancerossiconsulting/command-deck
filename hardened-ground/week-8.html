Week 8  ·  The Call to Action

Recognizing the Visitation

Takeaway. God comes, and a people can miss the moment when he does. Those who recognize it are sent: “As the Father has sent me, so I am sending you.”

Opening question. What weather signs are you good at reading, and what present moment might you be missing?

Setting

We learn to read the time we are actually in, and we come to the call to action and the last word of the course.

Context

Jesus’ parable of the sheep and the goats turns on one question: “Lord, when did we ever see you?” (Matthew 25:37, NLT). He came in the hungry, the stranger, and the prisoner, and people did not recognize him. In Luke 19, Jesus weeps over Jerusalem for missing the time of God’s visitation. In Luke 12 he tells the crowds they can interpret the sky but not the present times. In Matthew 24 he tells his disciples to stay awake, to read the fig tree, and to be found faithful when the moment arrives. This is kairos work: recognizing a visitation when it comes. Then, on the cross, Jesus cries, “It is finished!” (John 19:30). The work of atonement is complete. But John doesn’t leave the disciples sitting in a finished transaction. On the evening of that first day, the risen Jesus stands among them, shows them his wounds, and sends them. The danger this week is a socialized faith that misses the visitation, or hears “it is done” as permission to be passive.

Reflection

  1. When did we ever see you? In Matthew 25:31–46, the King says he was hungry, thirsty, a stranger, sick, and in prison. Both groups are surprised. “I tell you the truth, when you did it to one of the least of these my brothers and sisters, you were doing it to me!” (Matthew 25:40, NLT). The visitation came in the faces of the least, and it was easy to miss.
  2. Jesus weeps over a missed visitation. Luke 19:41–44 is a lament before it is a scolding. The city had enough signs, and its categories had already set. The tragedy is that God came, and the moment was not recognized. Hardened ground can’t receive a kairos even when the kairos is standing in the road.
  3. Reading the sky and missing the hour. In Luke 12:54–56, Jesus names a skill the crowds already have. They can forecast the weather. They cannot interpret the time they are living in. Ask the room what we are fluent at reading (news, markets, moods, church calendars) and what present tense we may be missing.
  4. Stay awake. Matthew 24:32–46 gives a simple picture and a hard charge. Learn from the fig tree. Do not pretend to know the day or the hour. Be found doing the Master’s work when he comes. Here faithfulness looks like readiness. Leave the speculation alone.
  5. When the moment is called untimely. King was told that his work in Birmingham was untimely, and he answered that “wait” had almost always meant “never.” Brueggemann’s prophetic imagination is a way of seeing what the settled consciousness cannot yet see.
  6. “It is finished,” and what it does not mean. Name the misuse plainly. Many Christians hear John 19:30 as if God’s perfect work, being done, now permits them to be passive in the faith. That is not appropriate. The cry from the cross is a cry of triumph. Dietrich Bonhoeffer, in The Cost of Discipleship, warned against what he called cheap grace: the idea that because the account has been paid, everything can remain as it was. “Cheap grace is the preaching of forgiveness without requiring repentance…” he wrote. “Cheap grace is grace without discipleship, grace without the cross, grace without Jesus Christ, living and incarnate.” The finished work is what makes the following possible.
  7. From finished work to sending. Walk through John 20:19–23. The doors are locked. Jesus stands among them, says “Peace be with you,” and shows them the wounds of the finished work. Then he says it again, and adds the capstone of this course: “As the Father has sent me, so I am sending you.” He breathes the Spirit on them. The Father sent the Son into the world. The Son now sends his people in the same motion. For us, the cross is where the mission starts.
  8. Examined faith, active in the world. The Bereans “searched the Scriptures day after day to see if Paul and Silas were teaching the truth” (Acts 17:11, NLT). James is blunt: “Don’t just listen to God’s word. You must do what it says” (James 1:22, NLT). Salt and light belong out in the world, not under a basket (Matthew 5:13–16). We pray, “May your will be done on earth, as it is in heaven” (Matthew 6:10, NLT). If what we see around us does not match that prayer, we are doing it wrong, and it needs disrupting. Kierkegaard’s charge still stands: a culture can call itself Christian and hardly notice that the living thing has been smoothed away. John Wesley, in his sermon “On Redeeming the Time” (on Ephesians 5:16), urged believers to buy back their hours for God. The NLT renders that verse, “Make the most of every opportunity in these evil days.” In his journal, on June 11, 1739, Wesley wrote, “I look upon all the world as my parish.” A faith that stays only in the sanctuary is a passive faith.
  9. What does being sent look like in the twenty-first century? Don’t answer this for the room too quickly. Get concrete. Where is the public square in an actual life: a workplace, a neighborhood, a family table, a civic argument, a place where God’s will is plainly not being done on earth as it is in heaven? To be sent as the Father sent Jesus is to be sent toward people, across boundaries, in the power of the Spirit, with a soft heart. The thread still sounds: “Today when you hear his voice, don’t harden your hearts” (Hebrews 3:15, NLT). Then let the last word of the course be the sending word. Read John 20:21 aloud again, and stop.

For Discussion

  1. Why do you think Jesus weeps in Luke 19, rather than only warning?
  2. What “weather signs” are we good at reading, as a culture or as a church, and what present moment might we be missing?
  3. In Matthew 24, what is the difference between staying awake and trying to predict the date?
  4. When has something later proved to be a visitation that, at the time, looked inconvenient or untimely?
  5. How have you heard “It is finished” used, and has it ever licensed passivity in your own faith?
  6. What is the difference between resting in Christ’s finished work and retiring from his mission?
  7. If we are sent as the Father sent Jesus, what about his way of being sent should mark ours?
  8. What does being sent look like in the twenty-first century, for you, this week?
