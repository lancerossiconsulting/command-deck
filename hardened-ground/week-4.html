Week 4

Prophets and Resistance

Takeaway. The prophetic word is often resisted in its own day and later shown to be true.

Opening question. Whose hard word have you been tempted to dismiss, and what would it mean to give it time?

Setting

We watch how truth is refused before it is vindicated.

Context

Jeremiah 38 tells how Jerusalem’s officials rejected a word the city did not want to hear. Jesus, in Matthew 23, tells the leaders they decorate the tombs of the prophets their ancestors killed. In Acts 5, Gamaliel urges the council to leave the apostles alone and let time tell the truth. You can see the pattern. Dissent is resisted, and then, given enough time, it is often shown to have been right.

Scripture

Texts. Jeremiah 38; Matthew 23:29–31; Acts 5:38–39

“For you build tombs for the prophets your ancestors killed, and you decorate the monuments of the godly people your ancestors destroyed.”

Matthew 23:29, NLT

“So my advice is, leave these men alone. Let them go. If they are planning and doing these things merely on their own, it will soon be overthrown. But if it is from God, you will not be able to overthrow them. You may even find yourselves fighting against God!”

Acts 5:38–39, NLT

Reflection

  1. Jeremiah in the cistern. Tell the story of Jeremiah 38. The prophet speaks a hard word about the city, and the officials lower him into the mud to silence him. The word was true. Jerusalem fell. For now, just let the room feel what it costs to tell the truth before it is popular.
  2. Tombs for the prophets. In Matthew 23:29–31, Jesus says the next generation honors the prophet the last one rejected. They decorate the monuments and still inherit the same resistance. It is possible to admire dead prophets and refuse living ones. Ask the room where that temptation still sits in the church.
  3. Gamaliel’s counsel. Gamaliel does not settle the argument by force. He says, in effect, give it time. If the new movement is only human, it will collapse. If it is from God, you will not be able to overthrow it. He refuses to let hardened certainty do the work that only time and truth can do.
  4. Royal consciousness and creative tension. Walter Brueggemann, in The Prophetic Imagination (1978), writes, “The task of prophetic ministry is to nurture, nourish, and evoke a consciousness and perception alternative to the consciousness and perception of the dominant culture around us.” He calls the settled, numbed way of seeing that rules the day the “royal consciousness.” Martin Luther King Jr., writing from the Birmingham jail in 1963, answered pastors who called his work untimely: “I must confess that I am not afraid of the word ‘tension.’” A prophetic kairos often looks untimely to people whose categories have already set.

For Discussion

  1. Why do you think Jerusalem found Jeremiah’s word so intolerable?
  2. What does it mean to build tombs for the prophets while refusing the living word they still speak?
  3. When is Gamaliel’s counsel (“give it time”) wisdom, and when can “wait” become a way to avoid obedience?
  4. Where do you see a “royal consciousness,” a settled way of seeing, at work in the church or in the wider culture today?
  5. King was told his work was untimely. How do we tell a true interruption from mere contrariness?
