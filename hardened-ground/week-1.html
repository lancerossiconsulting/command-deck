Week 1

Chronos and Kairos

Takeaway. We live in measured time, but we are called to recognize and answer the appointed moment.

Opening question. Where in your life right now are you watching the clock, and where might God be asking you to recognize a moment?

Setting

We begin by learning to tell two kinds of time apart.

Context

The New Testament writers had more than one word for time. Chronos is duration: how long something lasts, one thing after another. Kairos is the ripe or appointed moment, a season that asks for a response. The Greek Old Testament often uses kairos for the Hebrew ʿet, the “time for everything” of Ecclesiastes 3. Jesus and Paul use both words with care, and sometimes set them side by side (Acts 1:7; 1 Thessalonians 5:1).

Scripture

Texts. Ecclesiastes 3:1–8; Mark 1:15; Galatians 4:4

“For everything there is a season, a time for every activity under heaven.”

Ecclesiastes 3:1, NLT

“The time promised by God has come at last!” he announced. “The Kingdom of God is near!”

Mark 1:15, NLT

“But when the right time came, God sent his Son.”

Galatians 4:4, NLT

Reflection

  1. Time we can measure. Walk the room through chronos as ordinary duration: a long illness, a span of years, time given to repent. Augustine, in Confessions XI.14, admits how strange this familiar thing is: “What, then, is time? If no one asks me, I know what it is. If I wish to explain it to him who asks me, I do not know” (trans. Albert C. Outler). Time itself, he says, is created. God is not one more item inside the sequence.
  2. Time that asks for a response. Kairos is about the quality of a moment, where chronos is about quantity. Jesus’ first public word in Mark is that the appointed time has come, and that the Kingdom is near. A kairos asks to be answered, and noticing it is only the start.
  3. When measured time opens onto a moment. In Galatians 4:4, the length of history ripens until God sends his Son. Chronos fills up until it opens onto kairos.
  4. Why this matters for the course. Understanding hardens in chronos when nothing interrupts it. The prophetic word arrives as kairos, a moment that asks for a fresh decision. A soft heart can still hear “today” (Hebrews 3:15).

For Discussion

  1. In your own words, what is the difference between chronos and kairos?
  2. Where do you live most of the week: watching the clock, or paying attention to the moment in front of you?
  3. Ecclesiastes 3 says there is a time for every activity under heaven. Which “time” in your life feels out of season right now, and why?
  4. Jesus announces that the time has come. What would it mean to treat this season of the church as a kairos, something more than a calendar of Sundays?
  5. Hebrews 3:15 says, “Today when you hear his voice, don’t harden your hearts.” What helps a heart stay soft across ordinary days?
