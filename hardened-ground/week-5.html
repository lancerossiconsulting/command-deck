Week 5

Jesus the Great Disruptor

Takeaway. Jesus does not leave settled religion where he finds it. He returns people, and their habits, to God.

Opening question. Where is Jesus still upsetting a religious habit you have grown comfortable with?

Setting

We watch Jesus turn familiar arrangements upside down.

Context

Mark gathers scenes in which Jesus revises what people thought was fixed: Sabbath practice (2:27), the meaning of greatness (10:35–45), and the claims of Caesar and of God (12:13–17). Each time, he is reclaiming what belongs to God. His teaching keeps moving, and it will not be fenced in by our conclusions.

Scripture

Texts. Mark 2:27; Mark 10:35–45; Mark 12:17

“The Sabbath was made to meet the needs of people, and not people to meet the requirements of the Sabbath.”

Mark 2:27, NLT

“Well, then,” Jesus said, “give to Caesar what belongs to Caesar, and give to God what belongs to God.”

Mark 12:17, NLT

Reflection

  1. The Sabbath was made for people. Read Mark 2:27 in its setting: the disciples pluck grain, and the complaint is that they have broken the Sabbath. Jesus will not let a holy practice become a burden that forgets the people it was given to serve. Holiness still matters. It is meant to serve human life under God.
  2. Greatness turned upside down. In Mark 10:35–45, James and John want the seats of honor. Jesus tells them that the one who wants to be first must become the servant of all, and that the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life. A cross-shaped way of leading cuts right across status, even religious status.
  3. The coin and the image. In Mark 12:13–17, a trap is set: pay the tax and look like a collaborator, refuse it and look like a rebel. Jesus asks whose image is on the coin. Caesar gets the coin that bears his image. God gets the person who bears his (Genesis 1:27). The verse does not remove us from public life. It limits what Caesar may claim. Conscience and worship are not his to take.
  4. Disruption in the service of an alternative. Brueggemann argues that prophetic ministry both criticizes the dominant way of seeing and energizes people toward a new one. Jesus does both. His disruption has a purpose. He is love that will not let a hardened system have the last word. Ask the room what Jesus unsettled then, and what he is still unsettling now.

For Discussion

  1. Why was the Sabbath question such a sharp test, and what does Jesus’ answer reveal about the purpose of holy practices?
  2. Where do you still measure greatness the way James and John did?
  3. If the coin belongs to Caesar because it bears his image, what does it mean that we bear God’s image? What belongs to God in an ordinary week?
  4. Does Mark 12:17 separate church and state, put limits on the state, or both? How do you hear it?
  5. Where do you feel pulled today to give to “Caesar” something that belongs to God?
  6. If Jesus still upsets settled religion, what habit in us is he most likely to reach for first?
